\begin{abstract}
We extend equity-pairs trading to relative implied volatility in approximately
30-day, 25-delta puts on related stocks. A screened opportunity rule generates
trades; learning then determines their entry size. In a simulated \money{10 million}
account over January 2022--August 2025, constant full sizing achieves annualized
excess Sharpe 1.055, compared with 1.263 for decision-tree states and cross-entropy
sizing. The observed gain of 0.208 has a conditional 95\% interval of
$[-0.103,0.424]$. The learner admits 110 trades compared with 75 under constant
full sizing. Performance varies across years, and previously inspected history,
different training and evaluation conventions, and simplified execution limit
the conclusions. The comparison supports positive historical profit above cash
and a higher point estimate for learned sizing, without establishing a repeatable
advantage from learning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Suppose AMD and NVDA face similar news, but AMD options become unusually expensive
relative to NVDA options. Selling the richer options and buying the cheaper ones
expresses a view about the relative price of volatility. Daily stock hedges reduce
immediate directional exposure, and initial vega matching reduces sensitivity to
a common small change in implied volatility. The trade still carries risk:
related stocks can experience different events, and option premiums may correctly
reflect those differences.

Our starting point is Ning and Lee's equity-pairs framework \cite{ning}, which
separates constructing a spread from learning how to trade it. We carry that
distinction into options. First, we ask whether a screened relative-volatility
rule produces useful historical trades under explicit contract and funding
assumptions. We then ask the central question: \emph{does learning improve how
capital is allocated to those opportunities?} A profitable signal and a useful
allocation policy are separate findings.

After explaining the motivating paper and our adaptation, we define the trade,
monthly training procedure and tree/CEM sizing method. We then compare constant
and learned sizing in the same funded account and examine the learner's annual
results. The comparison uses the retained configuration and saved historical
backtests.

\section{Motivating paper and adaptation}
\subsection*{What the paper does}
Ning and Lee \cite{ning} study how to construct and trade mean-reverting equity
spreads. For a pair they form $X_t=S_{1,t}-B S_{2,t}$ and choose $B$ by grid
search to minimize empirical mean reversion time: the average historical duration
from a significant local extreme to the next crossing of the sample mean.
This forms a spread without fitting an OU model to observed prices.

For trading, they use tabular Q-learning. The state encodes recent spread moves
by direction and magnitude; available actions buy, hold or sell a unit, subject
to the current position. The policy enters a long spread and later closes it,
rather than initiating a short spread. Its reward favors buying below and
selling above the equilibrium mean, with a transaction-cost term $c|A|$.
Q-values combine immediate reward with the estimated value of later decisions.

Because the reward requires a true equilibrium mean, the agent trains on
10,000 one-year simulated OU paths before application to historical stocks.
The market study forms ten US pairs in 2022 and trades them in 2023. Published
daily Sharpe ratios exceed the Distance Method and OU benchmarks for all ten
pairs. Those daily figures are not directly comparable with our annualized
options Sharpe ratios.

\subsection*{Criticisms that motivate our design}
Three issues limit interpretation. \textbf{Short history:} one trading year
provides limited evidence across regimes. \textbf{Costs and scale:} the reward
defines a cost term, but its numerical implementation and reconciliation to net
performance are not clearly documented. This is not evidence that costs were
omitted. Higher turnover raises total costs; larger or faster orders can also
increase per-unit slippage and market impact. A constant coefficient does not
establish scalability. \textbf{Simulation transfer:} formation is empirical,
but policy training still uses specified mean-reverting dynamics and a known
equilibrium. Real relationships can shift, so extensive synthetic experience
does not establish market transfer.

\subsection*{What we borrow and what we change}
We borrow the separation of opportunity construction from trading decisions,
and the question of whether learning improves on a simple rule. We change the
instrument from equity-price spreads to option-implied-volatility spreads,
using actual contracts, initial vega matching and daily stock delta hedges.
Completed historical trade outcomes replace a known synthetic equilibrium as
the basis for our main sizing learner. We make simulated cash flows and costs
explicit and extend the historical comparison across multiple trading years.

The next section turns this adaptation into a trading procedure: identify a
relative-IV opportunity, buy and sell specific option contracts, and hedge their
stock exposure until expiry. Our allocation method fits a regression tree to
completed historical opportunities, then uses the cross-entropy method to
choose an entry size for each tree state. Comparing this policy with constant
sizing separates the trading rule's historical performance from the additional
value of learned allocation.

\section{Our strategy and evaluation}
\subsection{From a volatility signal to a hedged trade}
Implied volatility (IV) is the volatility input that reproduces an option quote
under a pricing model. Realized volatility (RV) measures subsequent stock-price
variation. High IV alone does not establish mispricing: an expensive option may
compensate for large future moves. Our proposal combines observable relative IV
with relatedness and completed-profitability screens.

We use out-of-the-money puts near 25 delta with approximately 30 days to expiry.
Earlier comparisons using the same entry and sizing architecture gave stronger
net results for 25-delta puts than for ATM calls or 75-delta puts. A separate
comparison of 30- and 90-day contracts on matched proposals also favored the
shorter maturity under frozen sizing. These experiments motivated the contract
segment used here; their scope is documented in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.
We hold this specification fixed when comparing sizing policies.

For pair $A,B$, define $D_t=IV_{A,t}-IV_{B,t}$ and rank it within the
252-session rolling history ending on the signal date. Above the 80th percentile,
the rule sells A's relatively rich put
and buys B's relatively cheap put; at or below the 20th percentile it reverses
the direction. Relative richness is measured against the pair's own history,
allowing for persistent IV differences. Although the percentile cutoff is fixed,
the corresponding IV boundary changes with the rolling sample.

We screen for related stocks using return beta between 0.85 and 1.15 and
correlation of at least 0.5. We then check whether historical simulations for
the same pair and proposed direction have positive average net profitability.
This check uses outcomes completed within the previous 91 and 365 calendar days,
requiring at least 21 simulations in the shorter window and 63 in the longer
window. If either count is too small or either average is nonpositive, we do
not propose the trade. These simulations may overlap and need not have been
taken by the funded account.

An outcome becomes available to the screen only after its simulation has
finished, strictly before the new signal date. For example, a simulated trade
still open on 15 June cannot contribute its eventual expiry profit to a decision
made on 15 June. The counts ensure some historical evidence is available;
they do not represent 21 or 63 independent observations. The screens complement
the IV rank by checking stock relatedness and prior net outcomes for the direction.

Contract quantities approximately match initial vega: the change in option value
for a one-percentage-point increase in IV. Table~\ref{tab:trade} is an
illustrative example of the initial positions, with each put at delta $-0.25$
per share and each contract representing 100 shares.

If A's IV rises by two points and B's by one, the local vega approximation gives
$\money{900}-\money{450}=\money{450}$ before other effects. This is an interim
mark change. In the backtest, we hold each option leg to expiry and adjust its
stock delta hedge daily, unwinding the hedge after settlement. Option quantities
stay fixed after entry, except for documented corporate-event exits. Profit
includes premiums, settlement payoffs, stock-hedge gains and losses, dividends,
interest and costs.

\subsection{Simulating trades in a funded portfolio}
\label{sec:execution}
We evaluate each sizing policy in a single \money{10 million} account. The
policies receive the same trading opportunities and follow the same execution,
hedging and account rules. This comparison measures how their sizing decisions
affect net portfolio performance.

For each opportunity, we select the two put contracts---including their strikes
and expiries---using information available on the signal date. We attempt to
enter those same contracts on the next trading day. Quantities are rounded to
whole contracts, with no more than a 10\% difference between the initial long
and short vega exposures. A proposed trade is accepted only if usable quotes
are available and it satisfies the account's capital and risk limits.

Once accepted, the option positions are held to expiry and their stock delta
hedges are adjusted daily, except for documented corporate-event exits. All
positions share the account's cash and available capital. Stock hedges in the
same company are combined into a net position. Consequently, an existing trade
can reduce the account's ability to accept another opportunity.

Portfolio performance includes option premiums and settlement payoffs,
stock-hedge gains and losses, dividends, transaction costs and financing.
Option trades are recorded at the quoted midpoint, with an execution charge
equivalent to 0.10 IV percentage point of traded vega and a commission of
\money{0.03} per contract. Stock-hedge transactions cost one basis point of
traded value. Cash earns the benchmark interest rate; negative cash pays that
rate plus 1\% annually, and short stock incurs a 0.3\% annual borrowing charge.
Appendix~\ref{app:implementation} gives the cost formula and account limits.

Because unused cash earns interest, we measure the strategy's contribution
relative to an account invested entirely at the benchmark cash rate. This
separates the value added by trading from interest earned on the account balance.

Configuration selection compares 33 saved sizing configurations on 2018--2021
performance. The selected specification stays fixed for the later comparison,
while its fitted tree and size map continue to update monthly. Historical
examples before 2018 support the initial fit.

The later test covers 3 January 2022--29 August 2025. Configuration choices stay
fixed, but the monthly fitting procedure continues. For example, a trade
completed in March 2022 may inform an April 2022 fit; a trade that finishes
after the April cutoff cannot. This is a rolling train-then-test procedure,
not fitting a model using its future test outcomes. Signal generation stops
on 30 June 2025, with existing positions followed through the August cutoff.
The account carries its existing holdings and cash across the 2022 boundary.

There is a separate research-design limitation: 2022--2025 results had already
been examined in earlier strategy development before this split was imposed.
The monthly fits respect time order, but the test is not independent of all
earlier choices of contracts, screens and universe. We interpret the later
comparison as historical evidence rather than independent validation.
The historical trade outcomes used to train the sizing model were calculated
with a commission of \money{0.65} per contract. The final portfolio comparison
uses \money{0.03} per contract for every policy. Thus, the model learns from
outcomes calculated under higher commissions than those used to evaluate its
performance. Appendix~\ref{app:implementation} also documents the account size
used for configuration selection.

For daily returns above cash $e_t$, annualized excess Sharpe is
$\widehat S=\sqrt{252}\,\bar e/s_e$, where $s_e$ is the sample standard deviation.
We also report profit above cash, admitted entries, reserves and cash-relative
drawdown.

\subsection{Learning an entry size: decision tree and CEM}
The opportunity rule fixes the pair, direction and contracts. We learn only
the entry size: 50\%, 75\% or 100\% of a reference budget of \money{450} vega
per IV point on each leg. Opportunities differ in their historical outcomes,
so the learner groups them and assigns a common size to each group
(Figure~\ref{fig:tree-cem}).

Each monthly regression tree receives 27 inputs: 15 numerical characteristics
and 12 availability flags, covering relative IV, IV/RV, relatedness, maturity,
VIX, costs and completed profitability. It learns splits using historical
full-size profit above cash as its target. Each of its up to eight leaves is
a state. The cross-entropy method (CEM) then chooses one size for each state.

CEM scores each map using the combined daily profit paths of its historical
examples. For leaf $\ell(x_i)$ of example $i$ and its saved daily net profit
above cash $p_{i,d}(a)$ at size $a$, the score is
\[
g_d(\boldsymbol a)=\sum_i p_{i,d}\bigl(a_{\ell(x_i)}\bigr),
\qquad
\widehat S(\boldsymbol a)=\sqrt{252}\,
\frac{\overline{g(\boldsymbol a)}}{s_{g(\boldsymbol a)}},
\qquad a_\ell\in\{0.50,0.75,1.00\}.
\]
Daily paths preserve overlapping trades and zero-profit sessions. Constant-size
maps are included as anchors; the search does not establish a global optimum.

For a new qualified proposal, the fitted tree assigns a leaf and its size map
supplies the requested entry budget. Account checks then accept or reject it.
The tree groups opportunities by full-size profit; CEM chooses sizes by aggregate
daily Sharpe. Training sums standalone paths, while funded evaluation applies
the shared-account capital and risk constraints described in
Section~\ref{sec:execution}.

\subsection{Does learned entry sizing improve the benchmark?}

of the same reference budget. Each policy receives the same proposals and
keeps the proposed direction and contracts.

the learner's final lead develops during 2022; both policies lose ground
during 2024.

The main saved paired circular-block bootstrap, using 126-session blocks and
1,000 replications, gives a 95\% interval of $[-0.103,0.424]$ for the Sharpe gain.
It includes zero and omits search adjustment. The observed gain therefore does
not establish a repeatable sizing improvement. The learner
admits 110 trades against 75: it changes portfolio access as well as exposure
to individual trades.

The account earns annualized total return of 4.667\%, while annualized growth
relative to an all-cash account is 0.530\%. Most total account growth therefore
comes from cash interest. Trading is active---110 new entries---but its net
contribution above cash is modest: \money{248,673} over the later period.
Average reserve is 2.44\% of equity and peak reserve 15.77\%. We use excess
Sharpe and profit above cash to assess the strategy's added value, rather than
attributing the full 4.667\% account return to trading.

\section{Conclusion and limitations}
We compare constant sizing with a tree/CEM policy that learns entry sizes
from completed historical trades. Both policies use the same screened options
opportunities, hedging rules and funded-account constraints.

The constant-full rule earns positive historical profit above cash. Learned
sizing raises the funded point estimate, with a 0.208 Sharpe gain and
approximately \money{47,800} additional profit, but its gain interval includes
zero. It also admits more trades, 110 versus 75, under the same account rules.
Annual results vary, including a loss relative to cash in 2024. The evidence
supports a higher aggregate historical estimate for learned sizing, with
uncertainty about its repeatability.

These results describe a historical simulation on previously inspected later
dates. Training, selection and evaluation differ
in capital, commission and admission conventions, and execution and
option-exercise handling are simplified. The observed gain does not establish
repeatable improvement, live profitability or trading capacity. Most total
account growth is cash interest; the strategy's added value is measured above cash.

\section{Implementation details}
\label{app:implementation}
\subsection{Evidence behind the contract segment}
The earlier shared-architecture experiment compares ATM calls, 25-delta puts
and 75-delta puts using the same IV-rank rule, screens, tree/CEM structure,
cost conventions and funded replay. Its positive-size policy has stronger
historical net results for 25-delta puts. That experiment uses five-year
training windows and a large reference account, so its Sharpe levels are not
the final selected-policy results reported in Table~\ref{tab:main}.

The matched-maturity experiment holds pair, signal date, direction and sizing
fixed where both 30- and 90-day contract selections exist on the signal date.
It favors the shorter maturity for P25 in that historical comparison. It
transfers frozen sizing rather than independently optimizing each maturity.
Together these records explain our focus on 30-day P25; they do not establish
that this segment is optimal across all contract types and maturities.

\subsection{Risk, execution and accounting conventions}
Entry admission uses a 5\% stress limit, an 80\% reserve limit and gross stock
hedges no greater than twice equity. Inherited pair/wing checks use a
\money{1 million} reference account, while funded comparisons use
\money{10 million}. Reserve equals gross option premium plus half gross netted
hedge notional; it is not broker margin. Entry checks do not guarantee protection
against future losses or broker liquidation. Black--Scholes IV and Greeks use
a European exercise approximation for American equity options. Market impact,
early assignment and forced liquidation are not fully modeled; the execution
penalty is a simulation assumption rather than a measurement of live fills.

We book option trades at quoted midpoint and deduct a cash execution penalty
equal to 0.10 IV percentage point times the traded vega. For day $t$, the
evaluation costs are
\begin{align}
C_t^{\mathrm{execution}}
 &= \sum_{j\in\mathcal F_t}|\Delta N_{j,t}|
       \bigl(0.10\,V_{j,t}+0.03\bigr)
    +0.0001\sum_s P_{s,t}|\Delta h_{s,t}|, \label{eq:cost}\\
C_t^{\mathrm{funding}}
 &= \left[0.003\,H^-_{t-1}
          +0.01\max(-B_{t-1},0)\right]\Delta\tau_t. \nonumber
\end{align}
Here $\mathcal F_t$ contains paid option fills, $\Delta N$ is the number of
contracts traded, and $V$ is one contract's dollar vega per IV percentage point
(100 times the per-share quote vega). Thus $0.03$ is the commission in dollars
per contract. $P|\Delta h|$ is stock-hedge dollar turnover after netting, charged
at one basis point. $H^-$ is the previous day's short-stock notional, $B$ is cash,
and $\Delta\tau$ is elapsed calendar days divided by 365. The funding rates are
0.3\% annual short-stock borrow and a 1\% surcharge on negative cash.

Benchmark-rate cash interest is recorded separately as
$I_t=r_{t-1}B_{t-1}\Delta\tau_t$; negative cash therefore pays the benchmark
rate as well as the surcharge above. Expiry settlement carries no option
commission or execution penalty.

\subsection{Tree states and cross-entropy search}
The 33-configuration selection uses a \money{345 million} research account;
the selected method and constant controls are replayed at \money{10 million}
for the final comparison. This transfers a configuration across capital levels.
Training commissions are \money{0.65} per contract; funded evaluation uses
\money{0.03}, as explained in Section 3.3.

The 27 inputs describe spread rank, IV/RV levels, relatedness, expiry, VIX,
expected costs, completed profitability and availability. The tree targets
cumulative full-size profit above cash, with at most eight leaves. For $n$
completed examples, minimum leaf size is
$\max(8,\min(63,\lfloor n/8\rfloor))$. Fewer than 16 examples trigger full-size
fallback; the initial fit has 74 examples. Every available action requests
nonzero exposure, although account admission can reject it. Each month has
its own tree and action map; split features, boundaries and size assignments
can change while the 27-input specification remains fixed.

CEM searches assignments using completed daily trade paths, including zero-profit
sessions. Each of 50 rounds evaluates 192 assignments; the best 19 update action
probabilities with 30\% prior weight, 70\% elite weight and 3\% uniform exploration.
Constant policies and the incumbent are included; the random seed is 2026.
Training combines standalone paths, while final replay applies joint admission.
Selection and deployment therefore have account-size and objective differences.
