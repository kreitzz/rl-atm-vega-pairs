\begin{abstract}
We extend equity-pairs trading to relative implied volatility in approximately
30-day, 25-delta puts on related equity underlyings. A screened opportunity rule generates
trades; learning then determines their entry size. In a simulated \money{10 million}
account over January 2022--August 2025, constant full sizing achieves annualized
excess Sharpe 1.055, compared with 1.263 for decision-tree states and cross-entropy
sizing. The observed gain of 0.208 has a conditional 95\% interval of
$[-0.103,0.424]$. The learner admits 110 trades compared with 75 under constant
full sizing. Performance varies across years, and previously inspected history,
different training and evaluation conventions, and simplified execution limit
the conclusions. The comparison supports positive historical profit above cash
and a higher point estimate for learned sizing, without establishing a repeatable
advantage from learning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Suppose AMD and NVDA face similar news, but AMD options become unusually expensive
relative to NVDA options. Selling the richer options and buying the cheaper ones
expresses a view about the relative price of volatility. Daily stock hedges reduce
immediate directional exposure, and initial vega matching reduces sensitivity to
a common small change in implied volatility. The trade still carries risk:
related stocks can experience different events, and option premiums may correctly
reflect those differences.

Our starting point is Ning and Lee's equity-pairs framework \cite{ning}, which
separates constructing a spread from learning how to trade it. We carry that
distinction into options. First, we ask whether a screened relative-volatility
rule produces useful historical trades under explicit contract and funding
assumptions. We then ask the central question: \emph{does learning improve how
capital is allocated to those opportunities?} A profitable signal and a useful
allocation policy are separate findings.

After explaining the motivating paper and our adaptation, we define the trade,
monthly training procedure and tree/CEM sizing method. We then compare constant
and learned sizing in the same funded account and examine the learner's annual
results. The comparison uses the retained configuration and saved historical
backtests.

\section{Motivating paper and adaptation}
\subsection*{What the paper does}
Ning and Lee \cite{ning} study how to construct and trade mean-reverting equity
spreads. At time $t$, let $S_{1,t}$ and $S_{2,t}$ denote the two stock prices.
They form the spread $X_t=S_{1,t}-B S_{2,t}$, where $B$ is the hedge coefficient,
and choose $B$ by grid search to minimize empirical mean reversion time:
the average duration from a significant local extreme to the next mean crossing.
Formation does not fit an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck (OU) mean-reversion model.

For trading, they use tabular Q-learning. The state encodes recent spread moves
by direction and magnitude; available actions buy, hold or sell a unit, subject
to the current position. The policy enters a long spread and later closes it,
and does not initiate a short spread. Its reward favors buying below and
selling above the equilibrium mean, with a transaction-cost term $c|A|$,
where $c$ is the cost coefficient and $|A|$ is the magnitude of the trading action.
Q-values combine immediate reward with the estimated value of later decisions.

Because the reward requires a true equilibrium mean, the agent trains on
10,000 one-year simulated OU paths before application to historical stocks.
The market study forms ten US pairs in 2022 and trades them in 2023. Published
daily Sharpe ratios exceed the Distance Method and OU benchmarks for all ten
pairs. Those daily figures are not directly comparable with our annualized
options Sharpe ratios.

\subsection*{Criticisms that motivate our design}
Three issues limit interpretation. \textbf{Short history:} one trading year
provides limited evidence across regimes. \textbf{Costs and scale:} the reward
defines a cost term, but its numerical implementation and reconciliation to net
performance are not clearly documented. This is not evidence that costs were
omitted. Higher turnover raises total costs; larger or faster orders can also
increase per-unit slippage and market impact. A constant coefficient does not establish scalability. \textbf{Simulation transfer:} formation is empirical,
but policy training still uses specified mean-reverting dynamics and a known
equilibrium. Real relationships can shift, so extensive synthetic experience
does not establish market transfer.

\subsection*{What we borrow and what we change}
We borrow the separation of opportunity construction from trading decisions,
and the question of whether learning improves on a simple rule. We change the
instrument from equity-price spreads to option-implied-volatility spreads,
using actual contracts, initial vega matching and daily stock delta hedges.
Completed historical outcomes replace a known synthetic equilibrium for
training. We make simulated costs explicit and compare multiple trading years.

The next section defines relative-IV opportunities, option contracts and
hedging through expiry. Our regression tree groups completed historical
opportunities; cross-entropy search chooses an entry size for each group.
This is supervised partitioning followed by sizing search, rather than
sequential Q-learning. Comparing with constant sizing measures the additional
historical value of learned allocation.

\section{Our strategy and evaluation}
\subsection{From a volatility signal to a hedged trade}
Implied volatility (IV) reproduces an option quote under a pricing model;
realized volatility (RV) measures stock-price variation. RV inputs use trailing
data available at the signal date, not future outcomes. We trade puts near
25 delta and 30 days to expiry (P25), a contract choice retained after
exploratory research and fixed across all sizing policies. We do not claim
that this segment is optimal. Appendix~\ref{app:implementation} gives selection
and quote rules.

For stocks $A$ and $B$, let $IV_{A,t}$ and $IV_{B,t}$ be their selected
options' implied volatilities on signal date $t$. Their difference,
$D_t=IV_{A,t}-IV_{B,t}$, measures which stock's option has higher quoted IV.
We rank $D_t$ within the 252-session rolling history ending on the signal date,
requiring at least 63 observed differences. Above the 80th percentile,
the rule sells A's relatively rich put
and buys B's relatively cheap put; at or below the 20th percentile it reverses
the direction. The percentile compares the pair with its own history, allowing for persistent
IV differences; the corresponding IV boundary moves with the rolling sample.

Each month, we estimate beta of A's daily returns on B's and their correlation
using the 126 sessions preceding the month's first session. We require beta
between 0.85 and 1.15 and correlation of at least 0.5. We then check whether historical simulations for
the same pair and proposed direction have positive average net profit per unit
of initial vega. These are full-size, standalone hedged simulations on available
candidate dates, calculated with the training costs.
This check uses outcomes completed within the previous 91 and 365 calendar days,
requiring at least 21 simulations in the shorter window and 63 in the longer
window. If either count is too small or either average is nonpositive, we do
not propose the trade. These simulations may overlap and need not have been
taken by the funded account.

Only simulations completed strictly before the signal date contribute.
For example, a trade still open on 15 June cannot supply its eventual expiry
profit to that day's decision. The overlapping outcomes are not independent
observations.

Contract quantities approximately match initial vega: the change in option value
for a one-percentage-point increase in IV. Table~\ref{tab:trade} is an
illustrative example of the initial positions, with each put at delta $-0.25$
per share and each contract representing 100 shares.

The example matches local vega exposure, not the two stocks' eventual risks.
We hold options to expiry and adjust stock delta hedges daily. Final profit
combines entry premiums, settlement and hedges along the realized stock paths,
after costs and financing. We evaluate this complete trade; interim IV
convergence alone does not determine expiry profit.

\subsection{Simulating trades in a funded portfolio}
\label{sec:execution}
We evaluate each sizing policy in a single \money{10 million} account. The
policies receive the same trading opportunities and follow the same execution,
hedging and account rules. This comparison measures how their sizing decisions
affect net portfolio performance.

For each opportunity, we select the two put contracts---including their strikes
and expiries---using information available on the signal date. We attempt to
enter those same contracts on the next trading day. Quantities are rounded to
whole contracts, with no more than a 10\% difference between the initial long
and short vega exposures. A proposed trade is accepted only if usable quotes
are available and it satisfies the account's capital and risk limits. The common opportunity
set comes from the retained historical formation calendar and pair panel
listed in Appendix~\ref{app:universe}.

Once accepted, the option positions are held to expiry and their stock delta
hedges are adjusted daily, except for corporate-event exits described in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}. All
positions share the account's cash and available capital. Stock hedges in the
same company are combined into a net position. Consequently, an existing trade
can reduce the account's ability to accept another opportunity.

Portfolio performance includes option premiums and settlement payoffs,
stock-hedge gains and losses, dividends, transaction costs and financing.
Option trades are recorded at the quoted midpoint, with an execution charge
equivalent to 0.10 IV percentage point of traded vega and a commission of
\money{0.03} per contract. Stock-hedge transactions cost one basis point of
traded value. Cash earns the benchmark interest rate; negative cash pays that
rate plus 1\% annually, and short stock incurs a 0.3\% annual borrowing charge.
Appendix~\ref{app:implementation} gives the cost formula and account limits.

Because unused cash earns interest, we measure the strategy's contribution
relative to an account invested entirely at the benchmark cash rate. This
separates the value added by trading from interest earned on the account balance.

We retain the configuration with the highest annualized daily excess Sharpe
on 2018--2021 among 33 existing configurations spanning training-history,
feature and sizing-action choices. The selected configuration uses 27 inputs,
an 18-month window and 50/75/100\% actions. Candidate identifiers break score ties; its selection score is 0.575 in a
\money{345 million} research account. This configuration is then replayed at
\money{10 million}; the account assumptions are summarized in
Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

Each monthly fit uses candidates that passed the entry screens on their signal
dates, have valid simulated outcomes, and finished before fitting. These
examples need not have been accepted by the funded account. Only signal dates
within the preceding 18 months enter the fit. Tree fitting and CEM use this
same sample; the wider candidate history supplies the profitability screens.

The later comparison runs from 3 January 2022 to 29 August 2025. The model
specification stays fixed while monthly refitting continues: a completed March
trade can inform an April fit, but an unfinished outcome cannot. New signals
stop on 30 June 2025. All accounts continue from 2018 with their own accumulated
cash and holdings; the 2022 boundary is a reporting split, not an account reset.

Later history had already been examined during strategy development. Monthly
fits respect time order, but this period is not independent of earlier choices
of contracts, screens and universe. Training also uses \money{0.65} commission
per option contract, compared with \money{0.03} for every policy in final
evaluation. We therefore interpret the comparison as historical evidence.

We report annualized Sharpe of daily returns above the cash benchmark, profit
above cash and cash-relative drawdown. Appendix~\ref{app:metrics} defines
these metrics and the opening portfolios at the reporting boundary.

\subsection{Learning an entry size: decision tree and CEM}
The opportunity rule fixes the pair, direction and contracts. We learn only
the entry size: 50\%, 75\% or 100\% of a reference budget of \money{450} vega
per IV point on each leg, scaled by current account equity relative to its
initial equity. Opportunities differ in their historical outcomes,
so the learner groups them and assigns a common size to each group
(Figure~\ref{fig:tree-cem}).

Each monthly regression tree receives 27 inputs: 15 numerical characteristics
and 12 availability flags, covering relative IV, trailing realized variance,
relatedness, maturity, the CBOE Volatility Index (VIX), costs and completed
profitability. Appendix~\ref{app:features} defines the inputs. It learns splits using historical
full-size profit above cash as its target, splitting to reduce squared
prediction error \cite{tree}. Each of its up to eight leaves is
a state. The cross-entropy method (CEM) \cite{cem} then chooses one size for each state.

\textbf{How CEM scores a size map}

A candidate map $\boldsymbol m$ assigns an entry size $a_\ell$---50\%, 75\% or
100\% of the reference budget---to each tree state $\ell$. For training example
$i$, let $x_i$ denote its 27 inputs and $\ell(x_i)$ the fitted tree's state.
Let $p_{i,d}(a)$ be its net profit above cash on day $d$ at size $a$.
Each size has its own rounded contract quantities and standalone cash and
hedge flows; smaller-size outcomes are not proportional rescalings of the
full-size outcome. Stored paths share a fixed normalization, which cancels
from the Sharpe score.

We sum the daily profits across the completed training examples:
\[
g_d(\boldsymbol m)=\sum_i p_{i,d}\!\left(a_{\ell(x_i)}\right).
\]
Here $g_d(\boldsymbol m)$ is combined profit above cash on day $d$. We rank
maps using
\[
\widehat S_{\mathrm{train}}(\boldsymbol m)=\sqrt{252}\,
\frac{\overline{g(\boldsymbol m)}}{s_{g(\boldsymbol m)}}.
\]
The numerator is mean daily profit over the sample's trading calendar; the
denominator is its sample standard deviation. The factor $\sqrt{252}$
annualizes using 252 trading days per year. Overlapping trades and zero-profit
sessions remain in the calculation. This is a training score based on
standalone profit paths; the final account Sharpe uses percentage returns
and applies joint admission constraints.

CEM repeatedly samples maps, ranks them and updates the probability of each
size in each state toward the best 19 of 192 maps. Each round also includes
the three constant-size maps and the best map found so far in that monthly
search. After 50 rounds we retain the best-scoring map, without claiming a
global optimum. For each new qualified proposal, the tree assigns its state,
the map requests its size, and the funded account accepts or rejects it.

\section{Benchmark and learned-sizing results}
\subsection{Does learned entry sizing improve the benchmark?}
We compare the tree/CEM policy with constant requests of 50\%, 75\% and 100\%
of the same reference budget. Each policy receives the same proposals and
keeps the proposed direction and contracts.

the learner's final lead develops during 2022; both policies lose ground
during 2024.

The paired circular-block bootstrap, using 126-session blocks and
1,000 replications, gives a 95\% interval of $[-0.103,0.424]$ for the Sharpe gain.
It includes zero and omits search adjustment. The observed gain therefore does
not establish a repeatable sizing improvement. The learner
admits 110 trades against 75. The comparison measures the combined effect
of requested sizes and the portfolios admitted under the account rules;
it does not isolate gains from sizing trades common to both policies.

The account earns annualized total return of 4.667\%, while annualized growth
relative to an all-cash account is 0.530\%. Most total account growth therefore
comes from cash interest. Trading is active---110 new entries---but its net
contribution above cash is modest: \money{248,673} over the later period.
Average reserve is 2.44\% of equity and peak reserve 15.77\%; stress and
pair limits can reject entries even with a low account reserve. We use excess
Sharpe and profit above cash to assess the strategy's added value, rather than
attributing the full 4.667\% account return to trading.

\subsection{How does performance vary across years?}
Table~\ref{tab:annual} breaks the learner's later performance into calendar

\section{Conclusion and limitations}
We compare constant sizing with a tree/CEM policy that learns entry sizes
from completed historical trades. Both policies use the same screened options
opportunities, hedging rules and funded-account constraints.

The constant-full rule earns positive historical profit above cash. Learned
sizing raises the funded point estimate, with a 0.208 Sharpe gain and
approximately \money{47,800} additional profit, but its gain interval includes
zero. It also admits more trades, 110 versus 75, under the same account rules.
Annual results vary, including a loss relative to cash in 2024. The evidence
supports a higher aggregate historical estimate for learned sizing, with
uncertainty about its repeatability.

The comparison uses previously inspected history and differs across training,
selection and evaluation in capital, costs and admission. Execution and option
exercise are simplified. These results establish neither repeatable learning
benefits nor live profitability or capacity. Most account growth is cash
interest; trading value is measured above cash.

\section{Implementation details}
\label{app:implementation}
\subsection{Opportunity specification}
The retained segment uses 30-day expiry anchors from the historical contract
selection, then chooses a put in that expiry with absolute delta closest to
0.25, within 0.075. Ties favor narrower relative spreads, higher open interest
and lower contract identifier. Quotes must be standard 100-share contracts,
with positive bids, noncrossed markets, open interest of at least 100 and
bid--ask width at most 25\% of midpoint. The 30-day expiry anchor favors eligible
maturities closest to 30 days; next-session entry additionally requires 7--55
days remaining. Identifiers are fixed on the signal date.

Relatedness uses 126-session daily simple-return covariance divided by B's
return variance for beta of A on B, and the corresponding correlation. Values
are calculated before the first session of each month and held fixed that month.
The IV percentile uses available differences in a 252-session window including
the signal close, with a minimum of 63 observations. Profitability screens use
completed full-size simulations in each direction, including available dates
that did not qualify as funded proposals. Their net dollar profits are divided
by mean initial leg vega before averaging; completion dates determine the
91- and 365-calendar-day windows.

\subsection{Risk, execution and accounting conventions}
The account checks the proposed portfolio after each entry. Stress loss is
the sum of each underlying's worst nonnegative modeled loss across nine
combinations of spot shocks of $-20/0/+20$\% and IV shocks of $-10/0/+10$
percentage points, including its delta hedge. It takes the worst shock for
each name separately, without credit for pair correlation. Stress loss may
not exceed 5\% of the applicable equity budget.

Reserve is gross marked option premium plus half the stock-hedge notional
summed in absolute value across names after netting within each name. Reserve
may not exceed 80\% of the equity budget, stock-hedge notional may not exceed
twice that budget, and gross vega is capped at \money{20,000} per IV point per
\money{1 million} budget. Global checks use account equity; each pair's checks
use \money{1 million} scaled by current account equity divided by initial
account equity. The additional contract-segment check has the same budget and
is redundant with the pair check in this one-segment study. Same-date proposals
are processed in stable pair-identifier order. These are research admission
limits, not broker margin or guarantees against later loss.

The simulation cash-settles expiry and supports verified integer splits.
Announced corporate actions or unsupported declared distributions trigger exits
of both legs of affected pairs. Missing entry quotes reject the proposal;
missing held quotes can be marked using last-known IV with current spot,
rates and known dividends. Black--Scholes valuation approximates European
exercise for American equity options. Market impact, early assignment and
forced liquidation are not fully modeled.

We book option trades at quoted midpoint and deduct a cash execution penalty
equal to 0.10 IV percentage point times the traded vega. For day $t$, the
evaluation costs are
\begin{align}
C_t^{\mathrm{execution}}
 &= \sum_{j\in\mathcal F_t}|\Delta N_{j,t}|
       \bigl(0.10\,V_{j,t}+0.03\bigr)
    +0.0001\sum_s P_{s,t}|\Delta h_{s,t}|, \label{eq:cost}\\
C_t^{\mathrm{funding}}
 &= \left[0.003\,H^-_{t-1}
          +0.01\max(-B_{t-1},0)\right]\Delta\tau_t. \nonumber
\end{align}
The following notation identifies the quantities in the cost calculation.
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lp{0.73\textwidth}}
\toprule
Symbol & Meaning and unit\\
\midrule
$t$, $j$, $s$ & Trading day, option-contract index and stock index, respectively.\\
$C_t^{\mathrm{execution}}$, $C_t^{\mathrm{funding}}$ & Execution and funding charges on day $t$, in dollars.\\
$\mathcal F_t$ & Set of option fills charged execution costs on day $t$.\\
$\Delta N_{j,t}$ & Signed change in the number of contracts of option $j$; absolute value counts contracts traded.\\
$V_{j,t}$ & Dollar vega of one contract per IV percentage point (100 times per-share quote vega).\\
$P_{s,t}$ & Stock $s$'s transaction price, in dollars per share.\\
$\Delta h_{s,t}$ & Signed change in the net stock hedge, in shares; $P_{s,t}|\Delta h_{s,t}|$ is dollar turnover.\\
$H^-_{t-1}$ & Previous day's short-stock notional, in dollars.\\
$B_{t-1}$ & Previous day's cash balance, in dollars; a negative balance represents borrowing.\\
$\Delta\tau_t$ & Elapsed calendar days divided by 365, in years.\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The coefficients charge 0.10 IV percentage point of option vega, \money{0.03}
commission per contract and one basis point (0.0001) of stock turnover.
Funding charges use 0.3\% (0.003) annual short-stock borrow and a 1\% (0.01)
surcharge on negative cash.

Benchmark-rate cash interest is recorded separately as
$I_t=r_{t-1}B_{t-1}\Delta\tau_t$, where $I_t$ is interest in dollars and
$r_{t-1}$ is the previous day's annual benchmark rate, expressed as a decimal.
Negative cash therefore pays the benchmark rate as well as the surcharge above.
Expiry settlement carries no option commission or execution penalty.

\subsection{Tree states and cross-entropy search}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lp{0.69\textwidth}}
\toprule
Stage & Capital, costs and objective\\
\midrule
Monthly fitting & Standalone \money{1 million} trade paths, \money{0.65}
option commission; tree predicts full-size outcome, CEM maximizes daily profit
Sharpe without joint portfolio admission.\\
Configuration selection & \money{345 million} shared account, \money{0.03}
commission; highest 2018--2021 excess-return Sharpe among 10 history/recency,
11 feature and 12 action/history configurations. Selected score: 0.575.\\
Final comparison & \money{10 million} shared account, \money{0.03}

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
All stages use a 0.10-IV-point option execution penalty and one basis point of
stock-hedge turnover. Selection and evaluation use shared accounts with
\money{1 million} pair budgets scaled by equity; fitting uses standalone paths.
These differences are retained limitations of the existing study.

The tree targets
cumulative full-size profit above cash, with at most eight leaves. For $n$
completed examples, minimum leaf size is
$\max(8,\min(63,\lfloor n/8\rfloor))$. Here $\lfloor n/8\rfloor$ rounds
$n/8$ down to an integer; the rule sets the minimum examples per leaf to
one eighth of the training count, bounded between 8 and 63.
Fewer than 16 examples trigger full-size
fallback. Every available action requests
nonzero exposure, although account admission can reject it. Each month has
its own tree and action map; split features, boundaries and size assignments
can change while the 27-input specification remains fixed.

CEM starts each month with uniform probabilities. Its update is 0.97 times
a mixture of 30\% current probability and 70\% frequency among the best
19 maps, plus 0.03 divided equally across the three actions. The seed is 2026.

\subsection{Inputs available at the signal date}

The model uses the following 15 numerical inputs. Proposed-direction quantities
are oriented as long leg minus short leg. IV and volatility levels are decimal
annualized values. Trailing realized variance is 252 times mean squared daily
simple returns over the latest 20 listed trading sessions, with at least 10
observations, including the signal-close return. Returns use the retained
historical return series (generally excluding dividends), with adjusted-close
percentage changes where that series is unavailable.

\midrule

strictly before the signal date. Future stock variation and eventual trade
profit enter outcomes, not decision inputs.

\subsection{Performance measurement and the reporting boundary}
\label{app:metrics}
Let $N_t$ be account equity and $K_t$ all-cash benchmark wealth at close $t$.
Both start at \money{10 million} in January 2018. The benchmark compounds
the previous available WRDS zero-curve rate nearest 30 days over elapsed
calendar days using a 365-day year. Daily excess return is
\[
e_t=\left(\frac{N_t}{N_{t-1}}-1\right)
    -\left(\frac{K_t}{K_{t-1}}-1\right).
\]
Annualized excess Sharpe is $\widehat S=\sqrt{252}\,\bar e/s_e$, where
$\bar e$ and $s_e$ are the sample mean and standard deviation of daily excess
returns, and 252 is the assumed trading days per year.

For a reporting period with preceding close $0$ and final close $T$, dollar
profit above cash is $(N_T-K_T)-(N_0-K_0)$. Relative growth uses the ratio
$Q_t=N_t/K_t$: annualized growth relative to cash is
$(Q_T/Q_0)^{1/Y}-1$, where $Y$ is elapsed calendar days divided by 365.25.
Cash-relative drawdown is the largest percentage decline in $Q_t$ from its
running peak within the reporting period, including close $0$. Reported
reserve percentages are reserve divided by current equity.

Annual-table account returns are $N_T/N_0-1$; above-cash percentage points
subtract the corresponding benchmark return $K_T/K_0-1$. These period returns
are unannualized, including partial 2025. Sharpe remains annualized.

The later period starts after the 31 December 2021 close. Each policy retains
its own earlier gains, cash and open trades; benchmark wealth is
\money{10.510 million} for all policies at that close. Figure~\ref{fig:performance}
subtracts each policy's own opening dollar excess wealth, so curves begin at
zero without resetting the underlying accounts.
